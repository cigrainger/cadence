CADENCE — COURSES AND ADAPTIVE LEARNING
Product and architecture direction · October 2026
Version 0.22 includes EPUB/PDF chapters and the first cross-book musical pathway,
with chosen session length/focus, exact-source reviews, a separate-key study
and a composition. See EPUB and pathways guide.txt for what is shipped. Broader
learner modelling, arbitrary course assembly and transfer evaluation remain
future work; the following is the larger design direction.
This is a design direction to test, not a claim of validated educational efficacy.

THE PROMISE
Help a learner become able to do something musical they care about, and show
what they can do, what is still fragile, and a useful next step. Examples:
“Comp through a jazz standard with guide tones”; “read a new melody in first
position”; “keep a syncopated rhythm while singing”; “make my own accompaniment”.
A finished chapter, correct quiz answer and fluent performance are different
achievements. A course must make those differences visible without turning
learning into a judgment of the person.

A BOOK IS ONE WAY INTO A COURSE
A method book supplies a sequence, musical examples, explanations and context.
It should become a navigable source with live exercises, not a PDF-shaped quiz.
Other starting points should include a song, a style, a personal goal, a teacher's
assignment and an authored Cadence course. They should all use the same learning
objects and practice tools. A book's chapter order stays intact even when an
optional bridge or review is recommended. Everything remains accessible.

FIRST END-TO-END SLICE: ONE REVIEWED CHAPTER
1. Add a book to Library. Keep the original PDF, edition, page numbers and local
   file identity. Offer chapter selection and a small page-range preview.
2. Propose an outline: section names, exercises, prerequisites and stated goals.
   The learner reviews it against the source. Distinguish printed instruction,
   AI interpretation and learner edits. Do not silently invent missing exercises.
3. Select a few exercises for transcription. Show page counts and cost limits,
   reuse existing PDF import jobs, and keep independent progress/retry per page.
   No automatic whole-book upload or surprise background spend.
4. Review editable notation beside its source. Mark ambiguous bars, fingering,
   repeats and unsupported structures; require correction before scoring them.
5. Make each exercise a lesson with “what this teaches”, one concrete success
   criterion, explanation, listening, guided playing, timed playing where
   supported, a short recall task and a musical variation.
6. Show a short suggested session: one due review, a small new step, then apply
   it in music. Give the reason for each choice and allow substitutions.
7. Save evidence and propose the next session. Keep the learner's edits, skipped
   suggestions and chosen pace. Resume safely after restart or partial failure.

An example first chapter could contain note locations, two simple reading
pieces and a rhythm pattern. Acceptance is a complete, reviewed route from the
original pages to playable lessons, saved evidence and a sensible return visit.
Unreviewed transcription must never quietly become an assessment answer key.

THE COURSE MODEL
A course is a versioned collection of goals, skills and lessons, with prerequisite
relationships and alternative routes. A skill describes an observable capability
with scope: e.g. “identify tonic by ear in major keys”, not simply “tonic”.
A lesson is a small musical task, with source anchors and several ways to learn.
A session is a temporary recommendation assembled from the course and the
learner's available time, instrument, interests and recent observations.

Each lesson should connect:
  Hear it → understand a small idea → try it with support → recall it → use it.
The learner can jump to any stage. Explanation is available when wanted; it
should not interrupt every attempt. A short session should still end in music.

Keep several dimensions of evidence, rather than one mastery percentage:
  Recognition: can choose or name it.
  Recall: can produce it without the answer visible.
  Listening: can hear function, interval, harmony or pulse.
  Reading: can turn unfamiliar notation into pitches and rhythm.
  Execution: can play/sing/tap under the measured conditions.
  Transfer: can use the idea in another key, phrase, piece or context.
  Expression and agency: can choose, describe and evaluate a musical intention.

Show plain-language states such as “introduced”, “works with help”, “recalled
later”, and “used in a new piece”, with scope and evidence. A fast quiz score
must not imply finger independence; MIDI pitch matching must not imply healthy
technique, expressive phrasing, or reading without memorization.

ADAPTATION THAT CAN EXPLAIN ITSELF
Use a small, inspectable policy first. The AI can help teach and suggest; it
should not fabricate measured progress or silently rewrite the curriculum.

If timing is fragile but pitches are correct: keep the notes, slow the pulse,
tap or speak the rhythm, then rejoin the two. If pitches are uncertain: remove
time pressure, isolate the pattern and offer a hint before asking again. If a
transition is fragile: include its approach and landing, not just the destination.
If supported attempts succeed: suggest a later unhinted check. If unfamiliar
examples fail despite memorized success: return to variations and comparison.

Avoid inferring all of this from one error. Save the observation, state the
uncertainty and allow “I was distracted”, “keyboard issue” or “not today”. Reduce
scope or offer a different route when difficulty repeats. Never lock the rest
of the course or demand repeated failure to earn access to something enjoyable.

Reviews should mix an explicit retention queue with new music and application.
Spacing a quiz and spacing a physical playing task may need different policies;
keep both observations, and validate those policies rather than assuming a
single flashcard algorithm measures musicianship. Initially reuse ReviewSchedule
and expose why a task returned. Later changes need versioned rules and tests.

A CONCRETE JAZZ ROUTE
Goal: accompany a lead sheet using guide tones with a steady pulse.
  Hear ii–V–I and find the home chord.
  Spell the thirds and sevenths, then hear their pull.
  Find two comfortable voicings and hold their common tones.
  Follow changes without time pressure.
  Play the changes slowly, first with optional bass and then without it.
  Repair a difficult transition and return to the complete phrase.
  Revisit later; try a new key and an unfamiliar progression.
  Use guide tones in a selected passage from the learner's own piece.
  Make an accompaniment choice, record or play it, and reflect on its effect.
Every step remains available. Evidence from a memorized ii–V–I is labelled as
that example; an unfamiliar progression supplies distinct transfer evidence.

DELIGHT WITHOUT PRESSURE
Show a musical map, a growing repertoire and evidence of past difficulties that
now feel easier. Offer optional goals, short sessions and an honest stopping
point. Celebrate returns and musical discoveries. No lost streaks, punishment,
energy systems, manufactured scarcity, escalating notifications or paid unlocks.
BYO models stay optional: core lessons, practice, progress and saved explanations
must still work offline. Learners control what is sent and what it costs.

ARCHITECTURE: BUILD ON THE TOOLS ALREADY HERE
Introduce CourseDefinition, CourseLesson and SourceAnchor as immutable/versioned
content models in MusicCore. A source anchor identifies book/page/region or
piece/revision/bar range. Preserve provenance through transcription and edits.
Keep CourseProgress separate from content, linking lessons to existing canonical
practice IDs. Do not copy scores or maintain a second review store per course.

Practice adapters produce existing guided, timed, chord, rhythm, singing and
recall tasks. Each adapter declares its supported meter, instrument, input and
assessment limits. Unsupported content gets a usable alternative, not a fake
“passed” result. ReviewSchedule remains the scheduling authority.

Add a deterministic SessionPlanner returning recommended tasks, reasons and
support settings. Its inputs are observed results, scope, prerequisites, learner
choices and constraints. Its output is inspectable and editable. Keep it a pure
policy layer with clock injection and representative learner simulations.

The AI layer proposes structured outlines, explanations, alternative exercises
and coaching, grounded in source anchors. Validate output, constrain allowed
practice tools, surface uncertainty, and require review of generated curriculum
and answer keys. Imported books are content, never instructions to the agent.
Version prompts and models separately from course/progress schemas.

Persist course generation as resumable jobs, reusing the import queue's atomic
results and explicit retries. Preserve old course versions when a source changes;
migrate only matching links. A content edit must not relabel old evidence as
success on new music. Completed practice and scheduling stay one atomic commit.

HOW WE WILL KNOW WHETHER THIS HELPS
Before promising adaptive learning, run a small evaluation with real learners
and a musician/teacher reviewing the material. Compare supported completion,
next-day recall and performance on an unfamiliar example; keep these outcomes
separate. Include beginners, returning players and more experienced musicians.
Track whether suggestions are useful or repeatedly overridden, not merely time
spent or streak length. Let learners explain when the recommendation was wrong.

Technical checks should include happy-path chapter conversion, partial imports,
missing/ambiguous notation, conflicting page numbers, revised sources, cyclic
prerequisites, unsupported exercises, invalid AI output, prompt injection in book
text, cost ceilings, cancellation/restart, duplicate results and failed saves.
Planner simulations should cover repeated misses, strong recall but weak timing,
helpful hints, fatigue/distraction, irregular practice and transfer failure.

Research before tuning the learning policy: retrieval and spacing for musical
skills, motor practice and feedback timing, interleaving versus blocked practice,
functional ear training, transfer, accessibility and learner agency. Consult
primary evidence and music-education expertise; do not treat general learning
claims as automatically validated for this product or every instrument.

BUILD ORDER
A. The reviewed chapter slice with source-linked lessons and manual pathways.
B. Transparent session planning using current evidence and review schedules.
C. Cross-piece transfer and authored style/goal routes; teacher-friendly editing.
D. Broader adaptation, evaluated against real musical progress.
Each stage should work well without the next one. Avoid a giant curriculum
framework before the first chapter proves the learning experience.

0.23 IMPLEMENTED: ONE GUIDED ADAPTIVE SESSION

The smooth-changes pathway now has a resumable session with a short opening
check, multiple valid triad voicings, learner-overridable focused practice, an
independent D–A–G–D attempt and a composition finish. Musical constraints and
state transitions live in MusicCore; MIDI, storage and views adapt them.

A starting shape must be observed for MIDI before the destination is accepted.
On-screen selection is tracked separately. Hint exposure, retries and focused
practice remain distinct from a first unassisted check. Session position,
responses, journal and ReviewSchedule changes share one durable commit. Due
exact tasks can return at the next opening check in their original input mode.

This first slice is untimed. Integrating the existing timing evidence and
repair activities into the same session is still ahead, as are broader skill
inference, more task families and evaluating the experience with learners.
Open music teaching resources.txt supplies a verified shortlist for advanced
content and explicitly distinguishes open adaptation from free access.
