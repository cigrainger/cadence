# Guitar picking and accompaniment

Captured proposal, not implemented. The goal is to turn a progression from the learner's library into an accompaniment they can play, understand and make their own.

## See and hear the pattern

Show string choices, rhythmic subdivisions, rests, accents and picking direction or optional classical `p i m a` fingering alongside linked notation and tab. The learner can slow the demonstration, isolate the bass or upper notes, and hear how the same pattern fits different chord voicings. Hand guidance is visible; playback follows the actual musical events.

Begin with a small set of reviewed, original examples: a classical arpeggio, alternating-bass/Travis picking and a strumming pattern. Treat these as musical examples with variants, not a claim that one fingering is correct for every player or style.

## Build the coordination gradually

1. Hear the pattern and tap its rhythm, including rests and accents.
2. Play it on muted strings to focus on the picking hand, with guidance adapted for handedness.
3. Hold one comfortable chord and keep the pattern going.
4. Carry it through changes from a reviewed library passage, isolating difficult joins before returning to the phrase.
5. Make an independent return later, including a different progression when appropriate.

Keep the score and tab central. Give a clear demonstration, comfortable tempo, a focused retry and an explanation of the next recommendation. Separate self-reported technique checks, rhythm evidence, audible pitch evidence and independent performance in the existing practice records.

## Finish with something of your own

Compare fingerstyle, alternating-bass and strummed versions of the same progression. Choose or adapt a pattern, change accents or bass movement, play it back, and save the resulting accompaniment as the learner's own arrangement linked to its source. The existing eight-bar creative finish is a natural starting scope. Preserve the original piece and the learner's edits.

## Assessment limits

A microphone may support audible onset/timing and note comparisons after calibration and detection have been validated for the task. Muted-string exercises primarily need rhythm evidence and an explicit technique self-check. Audio alone cannot reliably confirm finger identity, pick direction or which string produced an ambiguous pitch. MIDI similarly establishes played events, not guitar hand mechanics. Metronome or accompaniment leakage must not count as the learner’s playing. Show uncertain captures as inconclusive and keep a useful self-check route.

## Shared foundations, specialist teaching

Represent the picking pattern as relative gestures over a chosen voicing, then produce events for the existing score, tab, playback and practice engines. Keep the selected source, pattern and voicing identifiable so changing one cannot misattribute an old result. Avoid a second score model or gradebook.

Keep tuning, string/course layout, register and right-hand conventions explicit. Guitar is the first complete teaching experience. Ukulele can gain its own patterns and voicings; banjo needs deliberate treatment of variants, rolls and its short drone string. The shared model should support that progression without presenting guitar patterns as specialist instruction for every instrument.

First acceptance: a learner can see and hear each pattern, follow the gradual sequence, use a library progression, save a playable arrangement and find it again. Unsupported input remains explicit. Review exercises with guitarists, test relevant keyboard/accessibility actions, and cover invalid string targets, source changes, cancellation and failed saves before describing the pathway as complete.
